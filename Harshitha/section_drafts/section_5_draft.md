# 5. Data Modelling and Applications

MongoDB’s flexible document model gives developers room to shape records around an application, but that flexibility doesn’t remove the need to design the data carefully. The same information can be stored in several ways, and each choice can affect query speed, storage, duplication, and the work needed to keep records consistent. Recent schema-design studies therefore treat modeling as a workload-informed decision: developers need to consider what the application stores and what it does with that information [28]–[30]. This section reviews those design choices, explains how embedding, references, validation, and indexes fit together, and then looks at applications and workloads for which MongoDB may or may not be a good match.

## 5.1 Document modelling and schema-design choices

A useful MongoDB schema starts with the application’s data and its common operations. For example, an online shop may store customers, orders, products, and payments. Before choosing collections and fields, the design team needs to know which screens or services read those records, how often they change, and whether the data arrive in a steady stream or in bursts. MongoDB’s manual recommends identifying the business domain, user needs, application logs, and frequent queries as part of this process [31]. The idea is simple: build the schema around the work the application actually does, rather than copying a table layout without checking whether it fits.

Research on automated schema design offers several ways to make this choice more systematic. Sachdeva et al.’s SRank method takes an entity–relationship model and workload queries, then ranks possible document schemas using measures such as response time, storage, scalability, throughput, and latency. The authors report a hotel-reservation case study, but the available article record provides only abstract-level evidence, so its detailed method and numerical results can’t be assessed here [28]. Bansal et al. likewise describe a workload-driven approach that uses a conceptual model, application queries, and anticipated data volume to generate a logical document schema. Their e-commerce case study illustrates the kind of problem the method addresses, though its results shouldn’t be treated as a guarantee for other applications [29].

Hewasinghage et al. take a broader view by evaluating candidate designs across several goals, including query performance, storage, document depth, and collection heterogeneity. This matters because the fastest design for reads might use more space or make updates harder. Their method lets designers assign weights to these goals, but the resulting design is near-optimal for the selected criteria, not a universal best schema. The authors also identify limitations in their evaluation, including possible bias in the quality measure and synthetic data in some scalability tests [30]. Taken together, these studies suggest that schema design is best viewed as balancing the application’s priorities, rather than applying one rule to every collection.

## 5.2 Embedding, referencing, validation, and indexing

Once the main entities and operations are clear, the design must decide which related records belong together in a document. Embedding can be convenient when information is usually read together and has a manageable size. For instance, an order could contain a snapshot of its line items so that opening the order returns the details in one read. A reference can make more sense when the related data grow without a clear limit, are used independently, or change on their own. A product’s current inventory, for example, may need to be updated separately from the order history that records what a customer bought. MongoDB’s documentation presents these as trade-offs involving access patterns, growth, updates, duplication, and consistency—not a simple “embed everything” rule [33].

Roy-Hubara et al. describe relationship cardinality and query frequency as factors in choosing between embedding and references. Their method considers whether either side of a relationship can exist on its own, how often each side is queried, and how many related documents may be involved. Their MongoDB evaluation compared a reference-based design with a design produced by their method. For one IMDb case, the authors report fewer aggregation stages and lower execution time, but the total execution-time difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.118), and the evaluation used a small query set. The findings are useful for showing how functional requirements can shape a schema, but they don’t prove that one relationship pattern is always faster [32].

A flexible schema also doesn’t mean that every document should be allowed to contain any value. MongoDB supports validation rules that can restrict field types or value ranges. Validation can be applied when the application has settled on important data requirements, while leaving other fields flexible. By default, writes that produce invalid documents are rejected; validation levels and actions can change how rules apply to updates or whether an invalid write is rejected or only produces a warning [34]. In everyday terms, validation can act like a guardrail: it helps catch a misspelled or incorrectly typed field before it spreads through the application.

Indexes should follow the same workload analysis. A product page that frequently looks up items by category and price may benefit from an index designed for those queries, while a rarely used report may not justify the extra index cost. MongoDB’s manual recommends considering query types, read/write balance, memory, query frequency, and representative profiling when selecting indexes. It also describes field-order guidance for compound indexes, including the Equality–Sort–Range guideline [35]. Because indexes take storage and add work to writes, they should be judged against the queries the application needs—not added simply because a field exists. The detailed index types and execution behavior are covered in Section 4.2.

## 5.3 Application domains and unsuitable workloads

Document databases are often considered for applications whose records vary in shape or evolve as requirements change. Examples discussed in the literature include e-commerce, customer relationship management, recommendation systems, and social-media analytics, where an application may work with varied records and related data [36]. MongoDB’s current manual also gives examples for time-series collections, including IoT sensor readings, financial measurements, retail and e-commerce analysis, application logs, and infrastructure monitoring [38]. These examples show possible uses; they don’t establish that MongoDB is the best choice for every application in those sectors.

Empirical studies make the workload differences clearer. Ciumac et al. compared MongoDB and RavenDB using a mobile and web product-order application inspired by industrial IoT. The tests included basic reads and writes as well as bulk ingestion, joins, aggregation, and text search. Results varied by operation: in that setup, RavenDB completed bulk ingestion in 62 seconds compared with 85 seconds for MongoDB’s `insertMany`, and its full-text search test took 45 milliseconds compared with MongoDB’s 310 milliseconds. The authors also report MongoDB advantages on other tasks. Since the experiment ran on one host without replication, sharding, or distributed coordination, its numbers describe that particular setup rather than production-scale IIoT systems [36].

Consistency requirements can also change the picture. Ferreira et al. tested MongoDB, Cassandra, and Redis across multi-region cloud replicas with YCSB workloads. For MongoDB, the paper reports throughput reductions of about 95.6% in Workload A and 66.8% in Workload B when using the tested strong-consistency configuration. Those are large changes, but they belong to specific operation mixes, database settings, and a particular multi-region deployment. They don’t show that MongoDB can’t support consistency-sensitive applications; they show why the consistency level and deployment should be included in workload testing [37]. Kamsky’s TPC-C adaptation offers a related lesson for transaction-heavy commerce systems: a benchmark built for normalized relational tables had to be adapted to MongoDB’s document model, and the paper warns that narrow benchmark results can be misapplied to other workloads [21].

The available evidence therefore points to workload patterns, rather than industry labels, as a better guide to suitability. Rapidly changing document shapes, data commonly read together, and application-specific access paths may fit the document model well. Unbounded related data, frequent cross-document updates, demanding multi-region consistency, or heavy full-text-search needs may require additional modeling, services, or careful testing. MongoDB’s time-series manual identifies unordered or non-time-dependent records as unsuitable for **time-series collections**; that limit applies to this collection type, not to MongoDB as a whole [38]. Before choosing a database, teams should test representative data and queries, then compare the results against their requirements for consistency, storage, updates, analytics, and operations [31], [33], [37].
